Unifying Disney’s Digital Identity: Migrating 250+ Experiences to One SDK

I led a company-wide operations program to move 250+ Disney digital experiences onto the newest Disney Identity SDK in under a year. The goal was to replace a patchwork of legacy and custom integrations with one standard platform. That meant one identity per user, cleaner code, and a sign-in that follows guests from Disney+ to ESPN to everything in between.

Key results

  • 100% of experiences migrated to the new SDK in under 1 year
  • Approval time cut from 2 weeks to under 5 days through automation and a redesigned configuration flow
  • 98% of new integrations launched with zero P1 issues or rollbacks in their first month
  • Enabled high-profile launches, including Disney+ Perks, ESPN Bet, and Disney+ Fortnite

01

The Challenge

Disney runs hundreds of digital experiences, and over the years each one connected to Disney Identity in its own way. Some used old SDK versions. Others had built fully custom integrations. That left us with two problems at once.

An engineering problem. Legacy code and one-off integrations were piling up. Every custom setup was something we had to maintain, debug, and secure separately. We needed everyone on the same standard platform.

A business problem. Because integrations didn’t talk to each other, a single guest could end up with several versions of their identity. Sign-in didn’t carry between linked experiences. Someone moving from one Disney app to another might have to log in again, and some of them simply didn’t. That friction was costing us customers.

The new Disney Identity SDK solved both problems, but only if everyone actually moved to it. My job was to make that happen.

02

Defining the Problem

Mapping the full scope

You can’t migrate what you can’t see. So I started by building a ticket system and dashboard that tracked every Disney digital experience with an Identity integration, along with whether it ran a custom build or an older SDK. For the first time, we had one source of truth for how big the problem really was.

Making the case to 250+ teams

Migrations are easy to deprioritize, so I designed a roadshow and met with customer teams directly. I built the pitch around five questions every team would ask:

  • Who needs to migrate? Every team on an older or custom integration.
  • Why should they? Full feature parity, plus a stronger, unified user identity.
  • What is the process? Upgrade your code, get security and brand approval, test, launch, and a new unified identity is created and tracked.
  • When? Everyone migrated within one year of the new SDK’s launch.
  • Where do I start? A getting-started hub and dedicated communication channels.

Sequencing the migration with cohorts

All new integrations went straight onto the new SDK. For existing ones, I grouped teams into three cohorts:

  • Cohort 1: the biggest, most complex customers with the most identity traffic. I put the hardest cases first on purpose. Once our largest customers were on board, they’d clear the path for everyone else, and they’d deliver the biggest impact for guests.
  • Cohort 2: Teams already talking to us or interested in new integrations, plus teams with moderate identity traffic.
  • Cohort 3: Teams we hadn’t heard from yet, with small identity traffic.

Finding out what was really blocking teams

Before asking anyone to move, I needed to know whether the new SDK could actually do everything their old one did. I ran interviews with customer teams to confirm feature parity. Where gaps existed, I worked with engineering to prioritize the fixes. In total, we identified and closed 11 feature parity gaps, and I set up weekly report-outs so customers always knew where their blockers stood and when they could start.

03

The MVP System I Built

Rather than managing 250+ migrations one by one, I built an operating system that let the migration run at scale.

Visibility: two trackers, two audiences

  • Management migration tracker. Showed which stage each customer was in, how many integrations had moved, expected timelines for the rest, and who was blocked.
  • Feature parity tracker. Showed every gap reported to engineering: who owned it, its timeline, blockers and risks, and when it would ship.

Self-serve documentation

I owned the customer documentation hub and redesigned its information architecture so onboarding guides, implementation docs, and FAQs were easy to find. I also wrote a migration overview so teams knew exactly what they were signing up for. Whenever customers hit a new roadblock, I added it to the docs so the next team wouldn’t.

Support that scaled with the customer

I designed tiered support so every team got the right level of help:

  • High-touch support for Cohort 1. Dedicated support engineers, weekly engineering updates, and monthly check-ins on progress, blockers, and risks.
  • Launch support. When any team was ready to go live, Identity and support engineers helped test, confirm final configurations, and monitor through launch day so there were no surprises for guests.
  • Monitored support channels for general questions. Automated answers for common API questions, plus a dedicated monitor who pulled in engineering or product when needed.
  • Monthly open forums. A place to explain why the migration mattered and take questions from any team.

Automation: removing the waiting

The slowest part of every launch was coordination: getting tickets created, engineers assigned, and approvals from security, branding, and other teams. I automated it with two flows that began with intake forms:

  • Migration kickoff form. Automatically created tickets, assigned a support engineer, and added the team to support channels.
  • Launch-ready form. Automatically created tickets, assigned engineers, sent approval requests to security, branding, and any other teams the configuration required, and opened a dedicated launch channel.

I also replaced one-off approval threads with dedicated approval channels, so approvers were notified automatically and could easily loop in teammates.

Fixing the tool itself

Some delays came from the SDK configuration and approval platform itself, so I designed UX improvements for it. I made requirements clearer, cut the steps down to only what was necessary, surfaced key platform APIs for advanced configuration, and added automated notifications. Fewer configuration errors meant fewer rejected approvals and faster launches.

Leading Without Authority

None of the 250+ teams reported to me, and neither did most of the engineers, product managers, and approvers I depended on. The program worked because I kept everyone informed and made the right thing the easy thing.

  • Weekly engineering reviews to prioritize parity work, update timelines, flag blockers, and pull in other teams.
  • Weekly leadership report-outs with dashboard readouts on overall progress and key roadblocks.
  • Tailored communication for each audience: business impact for leaders, technical detail and clear priorities for engineers, and a simple, supported path for customer teams.

I managed a team of 5 dedicated support engineers and coordinated across 4 Identity engineering teams, 3 Identity product teams, privacy and security, and branding.

04

Impact

  • Every experience migrated to the new SDK in under a year, on target.
  • Approval time dropped from 2 weeks to under 5 days, driven by automated intake, routed approvals, and the redesigned configuration flow.
  • 98% of new integrations launched with no P1 issues or rollbacks in their first month.
  • Unlocked new 1st- and 3rd-party integrations across the company, including Disney+ Perks, ESPN Bet, and Disney+ Fortnite.

Beyond the numbers, Disney now has a single, consistent identity layer. Guests have one identity across experiences, and teams build on a shared, supported platform instead of maintaining their own.

05

Next Steps

With every experience on one SDK, we could finally see the full guest journey. I partnered with product, data, and engineering teams to design and plan an activity service that maps a guest’s identity and journey across every Disney entity connected to the new SDK. That kind of view drives personalization, retention, and revenue.

06

Reflections & Learnings

This was the biggest, most far-reaching program I’d ever led, and it changed how I think about scale. At 250+ teams, you can’t rely on heroics. You need systems: clear trackers, self-serve docs, automated handoffs, and predictable communication rhythms.

It also gave me a first-hand look at the technology engine behind a major media company, and how operational processes like Identity directly drive revenue and customer retention.

My biggest lesson was that influence depends on the audience. Leaders needed progress and risk. Engineers needed clear priorities and context. Customer teams needed confidence that migrating would be easy and that they wouldn’t be doing it alone. Getting the message right for each group is what moved the program forward.
